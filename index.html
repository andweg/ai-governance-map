Frontier AI Governance Map

As of 11 August 2026. Not anywhere near complete.

Physical chokepoints

Actor / InstrumentGovernsWhat it doesTeeth?
US export controls Chip designers, chip factories, cloud resellers. Indirectly: foreign AI labs A government licence is required to sell the fastest AI chips, and the machines that make them, to certain countries1 Strong. It's law. Fines, criminal charges, export bans, blacklisting. The rules also reach chips made outside the US if US technology was involved in making them1
TSMC / ASML and memory suppliers Every frontier developer A handful of companies are the only source of the most advanced chips, the machines that produce them, and the memory they need1 Strong, but private. They can refuse to supply you, or give your production slot to someone else. No law, no appeal, no process
Big cloud providers (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Oracle) Labs that train models, and companies that deploy them Check who is buying very large amounts of computing power, set usage rules, decide who gets capacity, and impose security requirements for protecting model weights3 Moderate. Depends on contracts. They can cut a customer off. But often they are shareholders of the labs they would be policing
Energy regulators and data-centre planning rules The build-out of compute Control grid connections, building permits, and who pays for new power4 Strong. It's law, but not specifically written for AI. Without a permit and grid connection, there's no data centre.5 This governs how fast AI gets built, not how models behave

Company self-governance

Actor / InstrumentGovernsWhat it doesTeeth?
Anthropic Responsible Scaling Policy Anthropic Sets danger tiers ("ASL levels"). When a model reaches a tier, tougher security and deployment requirements kick in6 Weak, voluntary. The company holds itself to it, watched by its board and trust.7 It only becomes enforceable where a law (California's for example) requires publishing such a framework8
OpenAI Preparedness Framework OpenAI Tracks specific dangerous capabilities and sets "High" and "Critical" thresholds that trigger safeguards before a model ships9 Weak, voluntary: a safety committee sitting at the nonprofit Foundation level can demand fixes or block a release10
Google DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework Google DeepMind Defines "Critical Capability Levels" for bio and chemical weapons, cyberattacks, manipulation, and AI that speeds up AI research, each with a response plan11 Weak, voluntary. Reviewed by internal committees only
Meta, xAI and Microsoft frontier frameworks Each firm Similar danger-threshold structures as above12 None, voluntary. Internal only

Shared voluntary promises

Actor / InstrumentGovernsWhat it doesTeeth?
EU GPAI Code of Practice Companies offering general-purpose AI models in the EU Sign-up commitments: document your model, assess risks, report serious incidents, and have a copyright policy13 Moderate. Voluntary to sign, but signing is the practical way to show you comply with the EU AI Act. No penalty for breaking the code but the regulators monitor signatories14
Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments ~20 frontier developers across the US, EU, UK, China and UAE Each promises to publish a safety framework with danger thresholds and lines it will not cross15 Weak, voluntary.
Frontier Model Forum Member labs (Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Amazon, Meta) Publishes shared technical guidance on danger thresholds, outside testing and security. Source of the field's common vocabulary16 None, voluntary.

Testing and certification

Actor / InstrumentGovernsWhat it doesTeeth?
EN ISO/IEC 42001 (AI management systems) Any organisation that wants the certificate Certifies that a company has AI management processes in place. Nothing about model safety, though Weak, voluntary. The certificate can be withdrawn, and it does not yet earn automatic credit towards EU AI Act compliance17
CAISI (US Center for AI Standards and Innovation) Volunteering developers Tests models before release (30-day pre-release review), with a focus on cybersecurity, and works on standards18 None, voluntary. It can test a model but cannot approve or block one. Its findings are not published anymore19
UK AI Security Institute Volunteering developers Tests models before release and does the underlying research20 None, voluntary. No power to approve or block the release
Independent evaluators — METR, Apollo Research, FAR.AI Whoever hires them Various evals on whether models can act on their own across long tasks, whether they deceive their operators, and whether they can meaningfully uplift a bad actor21 Weak, contractual. They publish their findings22 but are limited by confidentiality agreements and require the lab's permission to get access in the first place23

Government bodies

Actor / InstrumentGovernsWhat it doesTeeth?
European AI Office Providers of general-purpose AI models The single EU regulator for these models: can demand documents, run its own tests, order fixes, restrict a model, and fine24 Strong. It's the law. Fines up to 3% of worldwide revenue or €15M, and the power to pull a model from the EU market.24 Limited in practice by how few technical staff it has25
National market-surveillance authorities (EU) AI products on sale (not the underlying models) in the EU Each EU country's own regulator polices AI systems in its market Possibly strong, depends on the country. Fines and forced withdrawal, but only where a country has actually appointed and funded a regulator24
Department for Science, Innovation and Technology / UK AI Security Institute The UK Model testing (see above), risk assessment None directly. Sector regulators have real powers26
US federal: CAISI, executive offices, AI Litigation Task Force US Sets rules for government procurement, tests models voluntarily (see above), and sues states over their AI laws27 Strong against states, none against developers. The Justice Department can take states to court and federal purchasing rules bind suppliers, but no federal body can tell a lab how to build a model28
Cyberspace Administration of China Companies offering AI in China Requires registration and a security review before launch, review of training data, and labelling of AI-generated content29 Strong. It's the law. Can suspend a service or bar it from the market entirely30
